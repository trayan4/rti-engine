# National implementation status — Germany

**Instrument:** Directive (EU) 2023/970 (EU Pay Transparency Directive)
**Status as at:** 27 July 2026
**Jurisdiction:** Germany (DE)

## Transposition status

Germany has not transposed the Directive. The transposition deadline of
7 June 2026 passed without implementing legislation, and no draft text had
been published as of that date. A federal cabinet review was expected in
June 2026.

Germany is therefore exposed to infringement proceedings by the European
Commission under Article 258 TFEU.

## What is currently in force

The applicable national framework remains the Pay Transparency Act
(Entgelttransparenzgesetz, EntgTranspG), in force since 2017. Its main
features differ materially from the Directive:

- An individual right to information exists, but only in establishments
  with more than 200 employees.
- The comparator is the median pay of the opposite sex in a group of at
  least six comparable employees.
- Reporting obligations apply to employers with more than 500 employees
  and are tied to existing management reporting cycles.
- There is no obligation to publish pay ranges in job advertisements and
  no prohibition on asking candidates about pay history.
- There is no joint pay assessment mechanism.

## Effect of the Directive in the absence of transposition

Private-sector employers in Germany are not yet bound by the Directive's
provisions directly. Their obligations arise from the EntgTranspG.

Public-sector employers are in a different position. Where a Member State
has failed to transpose a directive by its deadline, sufficiently clear
and unconditional provisions may be relied on directly against the state
and against emanations of the state. This vertical direct effect applies
from 8 June 2026. It does not extend to private employers.

## Practical consequences for a request received now

- A private-sector employee's right to pay information is governed by the
  EntgTranspG thresholds, not the Directive's.
- The Directive's 5% joint pay assessment trigger has no equivalent in
  current German law.
- An employer responding voluntarily on the Directive's terms is doing
  more than national law requires, and should say so rather than imply a
  legal obligation that does not yet exist.

## Expected direction

The European Commission has recommended that the right to information
under future German law apply for the first time in 2027, with the
existing regime maintained through 2026.
